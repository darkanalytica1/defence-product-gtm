# The buyer is five people

In commercial software the buyer is often one person, occasionally three. In
defence the buying decision is distributed across at least five distinct
roles, which may sit in five different organisations, with different
incentives, different time horizons, and no shared inbox.

Selling without knowing which one you are talking to is the most common and
most expensive mistake commercial teams make in this market.

## The five roles

| Role | What they care about | What they can do for you | What they cannot do |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **The user** | Does this help me do the job, under stress, at three in the morning, when it is raining | Advocate credibly, tell you the truth about the workflow | Spend money, or commit to anything |
| **The requirement owner** | Does this address the documented capability gap | Shape how the need is written down, which is enormous leverage | Buy anything directly |
| **The acquirer** | Can I run a defensible competition and deliver on schedule | Run the procurement, set the evaluation criteria | Change what was asked for |
| **The budget holder** | Does this fit the plan, and what does it cost over its life | Fund it, or not | Usually, evaluate your technology |
| **The assurer** | Is it safe, secure, certifiable, and supportable | Stop everything, at any point | Champion you |

A sixth role, the **incumbent prime**, is not a buyer but behaves like a
gatekeeper in many programmes, because they hold the integration
responsibility and often the customer relationship. For a smaller supplier
they are frequently the actual customer. See
[09. Dual use go to market](09-DUAL-USE-GTM.md).

## Why this shape exists

It is a separation of powers. The person who wants something cannot buy it,
the person who buys it did not specify it, and the person who pays for it
cannot approve it technically. That makes corruption harder and makes
decisions traceable. It also makes the process slow and makes any single
enthusiastic contact insufficient to close a deal.

Understand it as a control system rather than as bureaucracy, and it becomes
navigable.

## What this means practically

**Map the five before you build a pipeline.** For any opportunity, write down
who occupies each role by name or by organisation. If you cannot fill in
three of the five boxes, you do not have an opportunity, you have an
interesting conversation.

**Different content for each.** The user wants to see it work. The
requirement owner wants language they can reuse in a document. The acquirer
wants evidence you can deliver, including your financial stability and your
quality accreditations. The budget holder wants a whole life cost picture.
The assurer wants documentation, and wants it early, because assurance
findings discovered late are catastrophically expensive.

**The assurer is not an obstacle to be routed around.** Engaging security,
safety and certification people early is the single highest return activity
available to a product team in this market, and the one commercial teams most
consistently skip because it feels like asking for permission. It is not. It
is discovering your real requirements while they are still cheap to meet.

**Enthusiasm from the user is necessary and not sufficient.** Every failed
defence sales campaign has a folder of glowing feedback from operators. That
feedback is real and it is worth having. It is the raw material for the
requirement owner's argument, not a substitute for it.

## A test you can apply

Ask, of any opportunity: *if everyone I have met said yes tomorrow, what
would have to happen before money moved?*

If the answer is a short chain of events you can name, you have a live
opportunity. If the answer involves a capability plan being rewritten, a
budget cycle that starts in eighteen months, or an accreditation nobody has
started, you have a development project that may become an opportunity later.

Both are worth having. Confusing them is what destroys forecasts.
