# From capability gap to contract

The document that decides whether you win was written long before the tender
was published. Understanding how it came to exist is the difference between
competing and shaping.

## The rough sequence

Practice differs between countries and between programmes, and the names
differ everywhere, but the underlying sequence is remarkably consistent.

1. **Something changes.** A new threat, a lesson from an operation, an ageing
   platform, a policy commitment, an ally moving first.
2. **A gap is identified.** Someone whose job is forward planning concludes
   that current capability will not meet a future need. At this stage the gap
   is described in terms of effect, not equipment: an inability to do
   something, not an absence of a product.
3. **Options are analysed.** Can the gap be closed by changing how people
   work, by training, by modifying something that exists, or does it need new
   equipment. Buying something is one option among several and is not the
   default.
4. **A requirement is written.** The need becomes a document with
   characteristics, thresholds and objectives. This is the moment that
   decides the shape of the eventual competition.
5. **An acquisition strategy is chosen.** Open competition, a framework
   call off, a collaborative programme with other nations, a sole source
   justification, a research contract first.
6. **The market is engaged.** Requests for information, industry days,
   published notices, supplier questionnaires.
7. **A tender is issued and evaluated.**
8. **A contract is awarded**, usually with options for support and future
   enhancement that may be worth more than the initial award.

## Where influence actually happens

Step four. By a very wide margin.

A requirement written around an outcome you are unusually good at is an
advantage no amount of proposal quality can replicate. A requirement written
around a technical approach you do not take is a competition you should
decline to enter.

Steps two and three matter almost as much, because that is where the
conversation is still about the problem rather than the solution, and where a
supplier who genuinely understands the domain can help frame what good would
look like. This is legitimate and expected in most jurisdictions, within
clear rules about fairness and disclosure. Those rules are real, they vary by
country, and violating them can exclude you from the competition entirely, so
know them before you engage.

By step seven, the useful work is done. You are competing on price,
compliance and delivery confidence against a specification you did not shape.

## The uncomfortable implication

**By the time an opportunity is public, you are already late.**

This is the hardest thing for a commercial team to accept, because in
software the tender is the start of the sales process. Here it is closer to
the end. If the first time you heard about a programme was the published
notice, your realistic options are to bid thin, to partner with someone
better positioned, or to decline and start building a position for the next
one.

Declining is a legitimate and underused strategy. Bidding to lose is
expensive, and a pattern of losing bids teaches the customer nothing good
about you.

## What to do about it

**Build a picture of the plan, not the pipeline.** Capability plans, defence
reviews, published strategies and budget documents are public in most
countries and are the closest thing to a forward roadmap of demand. They are
dry and long. Reading them is a durable competitive advantage precisely
because so few commercial teams bother.

**Be present before there is anything to sell.** Industry days, conferences,
standards bodies, working groups, research programmes. These are not lead
generation activities, and treating them as such is visible and
counterproductive. They are how you become one of the organisations that gets
asked what is possible.

**Publish thinking, not brochures.** In a market where you cannot demonstrate
most of your work, credible public thinking about the problem is one of the
few honest signals you can send. This is also, incidentally, why a portfolio
of serious written work is worth more in this sector than in most.

**Track the gap, not the deal.** Maintain a view of the capability gaps you
could plausibly address, and where each one is in the sequence above. That is
your real pipeline, and it looks nothing like a CRM stage list.
