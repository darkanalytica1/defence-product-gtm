# Commercial models that are not seats

Per seat and per usage pricing are so dominant in commercial software that
they are often mistaken for how software is priced. In defence they are
frequently unworkable, and understanding why tells you a lot about how to
structure a deal.

## Why per seat and consumption pricing struggle

**Budgets are fixed in advance.** A model where the customer's cost rises
with adoption asks them to commit to an unknown number against a budget set
before the year began. The safe response is to cap adoption, which is the
opposite of what you both want.

**Whole life cost has to be estimated up front.** Approval frequently
requires a credible cost over the full life of the capability. "It depends on
usage" is not an answer that survives that process.

**Counting users can be genuinely difficult.** Deployed, disconnected,
rotating and multi-nation users make seat counting an administrative burden
and sometimes a security question in itself.

**Metering may not be possible.** If the system runs disconnected in a
sensitive environment, usage telemetry flowing back to you may be
unacceptable, and consumption pricing you cannot measure is not a pricing
model.

## What is used instead

**Licence by site, platform, or enterprise.** Priced by where it is
installed rather than who uses it. Predictable, countable, and it removes the
disincentive to adoption. Often the cleanest answer.

**Delivery, integration and services.** Substantial revenue sits in getting
the thing working in the customer's environment, integrating it with what is
already there, and training people. This is real work, it is valued, and it
should be priced rather than given away to win the licence.

**Support and maintenance, usually as an annual percentage.** The steady
revenue that makes the business viable. Scope it precisely: response times,
what counts as a defect versus an enhancement, and what happens when the
underlying platform changes.

**Enhancement and development, against a contracted backlog.** The customer
buys a quantity of development capacity and prioritises within it. Common,
and it turns a roadmap disagreement into a prioritisation conversation, which
is a much easier conversation to have.

**Framework agreements with call offs.** A framework establishes terms and
qualified suppliers; individual requirements are then called off against it,
often without a full competition each time. Getting onto the right framework
is sometimes the single highest value business development activity
available, because it converts every subsequent opportunity from a full
competition into a much shorter process.

**Capability or availability based arrangements.** The customer buys an
outcome, such as a level of availability, rather than a product. Sophisticated,
increasingly common on the platform side, and it requires genuine confidence
in your own reliability because you are now carrying risk you previously
passed through.

## Structuring a first deal

Principles that repay attention:

**Price the whole life, not the entry.** A cheap initial licence with
expensive support reads as a trap and will be spotted. A sensible total with
a defensible split is easier to approve and survives scrutiny better.

**Separate what is committed from what is optional**, so the customer can
approve a smaller commitment now and exercise options later within an agreed
framework. This often converts an unapprovable large number into an
approvable smaller one with a path.

**Be careful with intellectual property and data rights.** Who owns
developments funded by the customer, what rights they have to your background
intellectual property, and what happens if you are acquired or cease trading.
Escrow arrangements are common and reasonable. These terms set precedent
across everything that follows.

**Understand the exit.** What happens at the end of the contract, what the
customer can do with the system, and what transition assistance you owe. It
feels remote when signing and it is the clause most likely to matter.

## The uncomfortable truth about margin

Margins in this sector are frequently lower than commercial software, the
cost of sale is higher, and the cash flow is slower.

What you get in return is duration. Contracts last years, incumbency is
genuinely sticky because switching cost is high and risk aversion is
rational, and the support tail can run for decades.

It is a different business model, not a worse one. It rewards patience,
operational discipline and the ability to survive long sales cycles. It
punishes companies structured around rapid growth from a standing start, and
that mismatch, rather than any failure of technology, is what ends most
commercial incursions into this market.
