# Discovery when your users are unreachable

Product discovery assumes access to users. Here you may have very little.
This document is about doing real research anyway, rather than substituting
confidence for evidence.

## Why access is limited

Not obstruction. Usually some combination of: the work is classified, the
people are deployed or training, their time is scarce and spoken for, the
procurement process constrains supplier contact during a live competition,
and the organisation is careful about who is given a picture of how it
operates.

Accept the constraint and work within it.

## Sources that actually work

**Former operators.** People who did the job and now work in industry,
consulting or teaching. Accessible, candid, and able to explain both the
formal process and what really happens. The limitation is currency: their
picture ages, and organisations change. Weight recent leavers more heavily
and ask directly what has changed since they left.

**Doctrine and published procedure.** How the organisation says the work
should be done. Public, extensive, and largely unread by commercial teams.
It tells you the formal structure, the vocabulary, and the intended
workflow. It does not tell you where reality diverges, but you cannot spot
divergence without knowing the baseline.

**Training material and courses.** Often the clearest available description
of a workflow, because it was written to teach someone who does not know it.
Frequently more useful than doctrine for understanding the actual sequence of
tasks.

**Exercises and experimentation events.** Where suppliers can sometimes
observe or participate. Expensive, competitive to get into, and the single
richest source of genuine observation available. Prioritise these over
almost any other business development spend.

**Requirement documents and market engagement.** A requirement is an attempt
to capture user need in writing, produced by someone whose job that was. It
is filtered and dated and it is real evidence.

**Published lessons learned and after action material.** Organisations study
their own performance and publish a surprising amount. This is where you find
out what actually went wrong, which is the most valuable information
available anywhere.

**Open academic and institutional research.** Defence research
establishments, alliance centres of excellence, and university departments
publish continuously on operational concepts and human factors. Much of it is
open access. Reading it consistently is a cheap, durable edge, and it is
visible: people notice when you are engaging with their literature rather
than their brochures.

**Adjacent civilian domains.** Emergency services, air traffic control,
maritime coordination, disaster response and critical infrastructure
operations share a great deal of structure with military command and control:
decisions under time pressure with incomplete information and distributed
authority. Access is far easier. Insights transfer more than you would
expect, with care.

## Methods that adapt well

**Study the artefacts.** When you cannot watch the work, examine what the
work produces. Formats, templates, reports, briefing structures. Artefacts
encode the process that made them.

**Reconstruct the timeline.** For a published incident or exercise, build a
detailed timeline of what was known when, what was decided, and what
followed. This surfaces where the delays and ambiguities actually were, which
is where your product should intervene.

**Test your understanding out loud.** Describe the workflow back to someone
who knows it and let them correct you. Getting corrected is faster than being
taught and demands less of their time, which makes it easier to ask for.

**Build something concrete early.** A rough prototype extracts more
information in twenty minutes than an hour of abstract discussion. People who
cannot describe their process will readily tell you what is wrong with your
version of it.

**Separate what you know from what you assume.** Keep an explicit list of
assumptions with how you would test each. In a domain with limited access,
undocumented assumptions accumulate silently and calcify into product
decisions nobody remembers making.

## The failure mode to avoid

The characteristic failure is building for an imagined user: a composite
assembled from films, marketing, a conference talk and one conversation with
a very senior person who has not done the job in fifteen years.

The imagined user is decisive, well connected, working from a large display
in a calm room, and interested in your dashboard.

The actual user is tired, working on a small screen over a poor link,
interrupted constantly, responsible for the consequences, and mostly wants to
know whether the thing in front of them is current and whether they can trust
it.

Design for the second one.
