# Glossary

Written in plain language, from the perspective of someone who needs to use
the term correctly in a meeting rather than pass an examination. Where usage
varies between countries and organisations, it is noted.

**Acceptance.** The formal act by which a customer agrees that what was
delivered meets what was contracted. Triggers payment and often starts
warranty and support periods. Define the criteria before you start building,
not at the end.

**Accreditation.** Formal approval that a system may operate in a particular
environment, usually with respect to security. Distinct from certification,
frequently confused with it, and typically the longer pole.

**Acquisition.** The whole business of deciding what to buy, buying it, and
supporting it through life. Broader than procurement, which is the buying
part.

**Assurance.** The documented, auditable case that a system is safe, secure,
and fit for purpose. Built throughout development, or reconstructed
afterwards at great expense.

**Availability.** The proportion of time a capability is usable. Frequently
what is actually contracted for in support arrangements, in place of
individual repairs and spares.

**C2, command and control.** Who decides what, on what authority, and how
their decisions become action and produce feedback. See
[04. C2 literacy](04-C2-LITERACY.md).

**Call off.** An individual order placed under a pre-existing framework
agreement, usually without running a full competition.

**Capability.** The ability to achieve an effect. Deliberately broader than
equipment: it includes people, training, doctrine, infrastructure, logistics
and information. Requirements are written in terms of capability, and
products are only one contributor to it.

**Capability gap.** The difference between what an organisation can do and
what it judges it needs to do. The origin of most requirements.

**Certification.** Formal confirmation that something meets a defined
standard, often relating to safety. Airworthiness certification is the
canonical example.

**Clearance.** Authorisation for a person to access classified information at
a given level. Takes time, is granted by a national authority, and is not
transferable between countries without arrangement. A facility can also be
cleared, separately from the people in it.

**Concept of operations.** A description of how a capability will actually be
used, in operational rather than technical language. If your customer has one,
read it before designing anything.

**Domain.** An environment in which operations take place: land, sea, air,
space, cyberspace, and depending on the framework the information or
cognitive dimension.

**Dual use.** Technology with both civil and military application. Also a
category in export control, where it carries specific legal meaning and
obligations.

**Effect.** What you are trying to achieve, expressed independently of how.
Requirements and planning are increasingly framed this way, which is helpful
for suppliers who solve a problem differently from the incumbent.

**End use certificate.** Documentation stating who will use controlled goods
and for what. Part of export licensing.

**Federation.** Connecting separate systems or networks for a particular
purpose while each retains its own ownership and control. The realistic
alternative to unifying everything, and a core assumption in modern
interoperability thinking.

**Framework agreement.** An arrangement establishing terms and a set of
qualified suppliers, against which individual requirements are called off
later. Getting onto the right one is often the highest value business
development activity available.

**Human factors.** How people actually perform with a system: cognitive load,
fatigue, training burden, trust, error. Consistently underestimated by
commercial teams and frequently decisive in adoption.

**Interoperability.** The ability to work usefully with other systems and
other organisations. Has technical, syntactic and semantic levels, and fails
most often at the semantic one. See
[06. Interoperability](06-INTEROPERABILITY.md).

**Mission command.** Leading by stating the intended outcome and the reason
for it, then leaving subordinates to determine how. Has direct implications
for system design, because tools that centralise visibility and approval can
undercut it.

**Multi-domain operations.** Deliberately combining activity across domains
so that action in one creates advantage in another, at speed. See
[05. Multi-domain](05-MULTI-DOMAIN.md).

**Obsolescence.** A component or technology ceasing to be available while the
system that uses it remains in service. A permanent condition in a sector
where platforms outlive their parts by decades, and a substantial market in
its own right.

**Programme of record.** An established, funded programme with an owner and a
budget line. The destination a trial or experiment needs a path towards if it
is ever to become revenue.

**Provenance.** Where a piece of information came from, when, and by what
route. Essential once information crosses organisational or classification
boundaries, because without it nobody can responsibly act on the result.

**Requirement.** The written statement of what a capability must do, usually
distinguishing a minimum threshold from a desired objective. The document
that decides the shape of the competition.

**Speed of relevance.** Being fast enough to matter given what the situation
and the adversary demand. A relative standard, measured across the whole
chain from need to delivered capability, not across your part of it.

**STANAG.** A standardisation agreement within the alliance context, covering
anything from message formats to procedures to physical interfaces. There are
many, they have versions, and implementation varies by nation.

**System of systems.** A capability formed from multiple independently
acquired and independently owned systems working together. The normal
condition, and the reason interoperability is a primary requirement rather
than an afterthought.

**Through life.** Everything from entry into service to disposal: support,
spares, upgrades, obsolescence management, training. Usually the larger part
of both the customer's cost and the supplier's revenue.

**Whole life cost.** The total cost of a capability across its life, not the
purchase price. The figure decisions are actually approved against.
